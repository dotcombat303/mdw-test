Instant Infrastructures

Yatharth

Open in its own page ↗

Curatorial note

Instant Infrastructures unpacks the invisible data architectures that operate behind a single ten-minute delivery order. It traces how disparate apparatuses—from residential access controls to state policing systems—converge to track, quantify, and discipline the bodies of gig workers as they navigate the city. Visualising this extraction within the platform economy, the work-mapping apparatuses are displaced not just spatially but across an asymmetric technical chain. Within this network, value is strip-mined by an array of stakeholders: the consumer, the platform, advertising agencies, the state and AI giants. The project presents an annotated essay and a 3D spatio-infrastructural mapping,; the project exposinges the data flows that operationalise the convenience of an “instant delivery" as surveillance and control.

Artist’s text

Ramblings: What is data work?

When I started working on this project, I had been fresh out of research on platform work in India, where my work looked at how gamification, standardisation and surveillance impact domestic workers and how it relates to larger social hierarchies. I was and am largely unfamiliar with the framing of data work.

While “data work” colloquially tends to refer to labour associated with data annotation, moderation, labelling and other forms of labour which involve working with “data” to feed AI systems for training, validations and other such usage; In my work I had come across a different kind of data work where the worker body itself becomes a data point to be calculated, manipulated and controlled. The entire premise of platform-based gig work is not possible without the datafication of labour. The gig work platforms and the state and private infrastructure which make them possible act as a system of datafication which enumerates a worker and their lives into IDs, performance metrics, gate access permits, mapping beacons, advertising displays and inventory prediction algorithms through networks of gGamification, surveillance, pPayments, insurance, telecom and ID infrastructures. This is not to say that the data work is only being done by the worker; every stakeholder, including the customer, is involved in this, but this is to highlight that work in gig work is data work.

While I was working on this project, images of garment and domestic workers were going viral; they were wearing head-mounted displays recording every movement, and they were being sold to AI robotic companies with a goal to one day automate and replace those workers. To convert the worker into a data worker and eventually to convert the work to data. While one may argue automation of manual labour is a pipe dream, it’s simply because the state-platform economy puts more value in the data and its potential automation than the actual bodies and lives which it intends to datafy and replace.

At some point you run into a problem: how do you create economic value by replacing someone who you never valued? There is not much profit left to squeeze. ‘Data-ification’ is this devaluation of bodies, work and being, an abstraction for manipulation, control and profit. This piece is an attempt to make this extraction visible in the context of instant delivery work.

I do feel the need to add a disclaimer that a significant portion of this project was coded and developed with an LLM. There is an irony somewhere, maybe hypocrisy, in the use of AI while producing work about data work and exploitation; the first people whose jobs got devalued and replaced were some of the champions of datafication, the programmers. Programming is also data work. As soon as someone benefits from sitting to enumerate, measure, manipulate and optimise any work, it’s data work.

How many data-points are generated in engaging with this artwork with friends? I don’t know. I am just thankful for your time.

​

About the artist

Yatharth is a researcher, designer and artist based in Bengaluru, India. His research practice explores caste on digital platforms and design injustice in digital technologies. Yatharth has worked with various nonprofits, research institutes, design schools and studios in the roles of media educator, qualitative researcher, curator, creative coder and designer.
