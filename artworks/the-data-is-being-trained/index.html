The Data is Being Trained

Debarun Sarkar, Cheshta Arora, Gabriele de Seta, and Luke Drozd

View PDF
Based on online conversations between January-February 2026, this zine gathers the folklore of a new occupation: data work. It gathersoffshore workers in Hyderabad and Pune to talk about their jokes,sayings, routines, and small acts that make algorithmic labour livable and contestable. A second strand documents a nonprofit effort where volunteers and activists trained a gender-based hate speech detection model in Indian English, Tamil, and Hindi. Together, theypush questions beyond the page for all of us: What kinds of life does this infrastructure produce? The design draws on the texture of ahate speech dataset collected between 2021-2023 as well as emergent folklore of Indian IT workers on Instagram. It plays with noise, signal and interference, and answers remain for the reader to piece together.

Data is being annotated

Cheshta Arora I read the proposal before this call, and I realised that the Uli project was the main thing that we sold during the application, but then we completely turned it upside down, and we made industry data workers the centre of the zine and the Uli part just became one box in the corner of the page.

I think initially, though, when we wrote the proposal, that was the only side of data work that we knew, or at least I knew, where I had worked with the annotators, and we had experimented with this new approach to machine learning that was going on at that time. This was five years ago when generative AI was not that big. The idea (we borrowed it from Waseem (2016)) was that while a lot of hate speech datasets and the detection models were being built, they were mostly being built by engineers or data scientists who had no understanding of what hate speech is or how hate speech or the experience of it changes from one individual to the other. Something could be hate speech for you, but it’s not hate speech for me. Also, the [women’s movement in] India has a long history of engaging with violence against women and hate speech, and online gender-based violence was an extension of that discourse into the technology sphere. So with Uli, the idea was not to use data scientists or engineers to annotate data sets of hate speech, but to use individuals or members of the community who are often at the receiving end of violence and also to understand if one could capture an intersectional understanding of gender-based violence. For Uli, we invited journalists, activists, and young users who were on Instagram creating queer content. The point was to invite them because they will have a more intuitive understanding of what can become hate speech in different contexts. So Uli was an experiment to see whether, when annotations are done with some context in mind and not objectively, does that produce very different results with machine learning? We did have some guidelines in that project, but we did not really force it on the annotators. We gave them freedom to annotate whatever they think could be hate speech in any context that they can imagine.

The point being that we first started thinking about this zine from that perspective, and then we ended up with data workers, with Aswin and others and then a different side of data work showed up, which was completely different from what that small group of people were doing at that time in 2023, I think, or 2022.

Gabriele de Seta Was this like a research project funded by a research institute or a small grant?

Cheshta Arora Yeah, this was a funded project to protect women and children in India from hate speech and violence. Lol. The experimental part was just added by the team because they realised that it’s not that easy to build something, especially something that is so contradictory by its very nature.

Debarun Sarkar When I heard about Cheshta’s project, the first thing I remembered was your work, Aswin. You used to work for a social media platform back in the day as a moderator.

Aswin Vivek: Yeah, I used to work with a lot of hate speech back then, yeah. And I was thinking about how this is relevant for even LLMs where you generate a story using an LLM. I tried to generate a story of a family in Kerala, and it always comes up with upper-caste characters. We did work on data annotation, but annotation is always subjective even though we work with a strict set of guidelines; we still get annotations that are vastly different. The companies that want to sell AI want to emulate the complexity, but I hope that we can still keep this to humans.

Cheshta Arora Yeah, and if I can also add to that, this project, Uli, it ended the machine learning part of it and now it’s trying to set up a data collective.

Aswin Vivek I get that, because otherwise there is a lot of casteism in this work, where people from a particular caste or religion end up being the manual scavengers on social media or AI supply chain or whatever.

Yeah, so when I saw the design, right, I was a bit shocked. I really liked it, but I was just wondering where that design came from? Like, was it like corrupted data or something? It just felt like that. It felt like a disruption of data flow inside of AI or something.

Gabriele de Seta Well, can you tell me why you were shocked first? It will be interesting before we tell you how it went.

Aswin Vivek Yeah, when I saw the design of the document, it just, like, the rush of shapes and colours, not colours, but the whole shapes and all that, it just felt like a shocker; it was like a ruptured data stream. I did not know for sure what you were going for, but it was beautiful. Yeah, like there is a beauty in, you know, in that noise.

Cheshta Arora Yeah, and there was also this question which was there in the proposal to explore how we can present the dataset in a very different manner. Datasets are produced for machines to read, and one of our questions was whether we could make them available to humans. So we visualised the token distance of some tweets from the Uli dataset and gave it to Luke. The point was to play with that dataset and show what’s happening, how the content gets broken down when the machine is trying to learn it. So that was one sample. And then there was a sample of social media posts that Debarun had collected, which was about IT workers ranting about their job and about AI and what the future of the industry is going to be. We shared that with Luke, and he brought it all together.

Aswin Vivek Yeah. I think it also represents the whole utter dullness in the minds of the people who work. I mean, there is a graveyard sort of feeling.

Debarun Sarkar The question of what to include and not include is a fundamental problem of folklore. I think if there’s brevity, it looks amazing; folklore looks really good, but then there is no context to describe it to the audience. If you go into a description of what folklore is in it, you end up describing it for a long time to make that thing understandable.

Gabriel de Seta I do think that Zine does a good job of showing it. As I said, there is the whole list about how people call each other. There are the social media screenshots in the middle that are quite obviously folklore related to AI and data work that show how people make fun of the systems or make fun of the labour situation, or they complain about things, or they give each other tips about, you know, how to make money next. So I think the folklore comes through. We didn’t write like a theory part, but to me, the zine is just like it should.

Debarun Sarkar, I think one thing that really showed that the interviews were successful and data collection was successful was when I came across the news article about something in Delhi where people are training AI for factory work by putting up more cameras on people’s heads.

Gabriele de Seta, yeah, what I find interesting, and back to folklore maybe, is that I realised that some things that we put in the zine now are, let’s say, old, because now everyone kind of knows about them. But when we did interviews and chats, they were kind of new to us, at least, some of them, because the workers are probably the first ones who articulate them as folklore, like hearsay, what is coming next, which kind of data will be prized, you know, like this video stuff, right? I think we heard it there first, and now we read about it in news magazines and other stuff that are kind of reporting on it. So maybe a good function of folklore is predicting or looking into what might be coming next, and the workers probably are the ones who know the most.

Cheshta Arora Also, I think, more and more people will recognise themselves as data workers because most of the job tasks are changing. So when a machine can do something, but you have a human in the loop, that human in the loop is essentially the one working with the data; not just to annotate or label, but also to understand how the model is working, or how I can prompt it differently.

Gabriele de Seta You can use ChatGPT for free, Claude for free. The only reason why they do that is that they want more interaction so they can use them to fine-tune the model and train a better model.

Aswin Vivek We had a lot of people who had always been working from home.  Some of them got sent to the office for the first time to train robots. You’re an IT sort of worker who works with computers, but you come out sweaty because you have been working to train the robot.

Cheshta Arora is a Senior Researcher with the Technology & Society group at the Western Norway Research Institute, Sogndal.

Luke Drozd is a UK born artist and maker currently based in Bergen, Norway.
